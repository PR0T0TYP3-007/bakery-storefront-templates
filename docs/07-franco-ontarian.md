# Template 07 — The Franco-Ontarian Bakery

Concept and art direction. The client's brief is a *"traditional french onatarian feel"*;
**"Two names for the same tart"** is our concept for it.

Merchant: **Boulangerie Tremblay**, rue Principale, Hearst, Ontario. A bilingual family
bakery, open since 1961, now run by the founders' granddaughter.

---

## 1. The reading, and why it matters

"Onatarian" is **Ontarian**, and that had two readings. The client confirmed **Reading A** on
22 October:

- **A — Franco-Ontarian heritage baking.** Sugar pie, butter tarts, maple, tourtière,
  bilingual French/English. Homely and rural.
- **B — a classic French *pâtisserie* located in Ontario.** Baguettes, croissants, éclairs.

**Reading B was the dangerous one.** An elegant French pâtisserie is Template 01 in different
clothes: a refined serif, a catalogue of precise cakes, a restrained palette. It would have
been the "same spirit" complaint all over again, and this time self-inflicted.

Reading A is a different culture with different food, and it gives the template an idea
nothing else in the set can have.

---

## 2. The idea: the two names are not translations

The strongest thing found in the research is a fact about the food, not the design.

The **butter tart descends from the tarte au sucre**. Seventeenth-century women in New France
adapted French sugar pie to what they had, which was maple; the English-Canadian version,
sweetened with brown sugar, became the butter tart. The Canadian Encyclopedia puts it plainly:
*"Butter tarts in English Canada are another version of this."*
([Canadian Encyclopedia](https://www.thecanadianencyclopedia.ca/en/article/tarte-au-sucre),
[Canadian Food Focus](https://canadianfoodfocus.org/food/raisins-or-no-raisins-canadas-superstar-tart/))

So *tarte au sucre* and *butter tart* are **relatives, not synonyms**. They are two things
with a shared ancestor, named on either side of a language line.

**That is the template.** Every item carries both names, and the language switch does not
translate the page — it changes what things are *called* and surfaces how the two versions
differ. Switch to French and the sugar pie is maple-sweetened and deeper; switch to English
and the butter tart is brown sugar, shallower, and comes with the raisin question.

**The raisin question is real and worth building.** The first documented butter tart recipe
(Barrie, Ontario, 1900) called for **currants, not raisins**, and whether raisins belong is a
live argument with festivals and trails attached. The product page carries it as a genuine
choice with a note about the 1900 recipe — a small, specific, true piece of content that no
other template could run.

**Why this is not Template 02's builder.** Template 02 draws a cake as you choose it. This is
a choice between two traditions that changes a name, a filling and a story — no live drawing,
no price calculator. It is closer to a glossary than a configurator.

## 3. The distance check (D23)

| | 01 | 02 | 03 | 04 | 05 | 06 | **07** |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Ground | cream | sun | plum | near-black | eucalyptus | cobalt | **`#a88a2e` wheat gold** |
| Accent | clay | grape | blush | acid | green | terracotta | **`#3f3a7d` wild blueberry** |
| Type class | serif | rounded geo | incised | expressive | grotesque | slab serif | **antique wood-type condensed** |
| Separation | hairline rules | drawn outline | apertures | overlap | ruled rows | tiled modules | **stacked enamel plates** |

Ground clears every shipped ground (closest 192 from plum, 196 from sun and from eucalyptus)
and 279 from Template 06's cobalt. Accent clears every shipped accent by at least 142 and sits
238 from Barcelona's terracotta.

**Two near-misses caught during planning, both mine.** A *currant red* accent measured **30**
from Template 01's clay — effectively the same colour. A *maple amber* accent measured **49**
from Template 06's terracotta, which would have made the two new templates share an accent.
Blueberry replaced both.

**Wild blueberry is not a compromise colour.** Northern Ontario is blueberry country, *tarte
aux bleuets* is a real Franco-Ontarian dessert, and Hearst sits in the middle of it.

**On the flag.** The Franco-Ontarian flag is green and white — green for summer, white for
winter, a fleur-de-lys for the francophonie and a trillium for Ontario, designed in 1975 at
the University of Sudbury by Gaétan Gervais with students, and an official emblem of Ontario
since 2020. It is the obvious palette and it **fails**: the flag green measures 142 from
Template 04's near-black ground. It appears instead where it belongs — as a real flag, in the
footer, on the *fête franco-ontarienne* (25 September) entry in the shop's year.

## 4. Art direction

**Wheat gold ground.** `#a88a2e` — grain, maple syrup held to the light, and the mustard of
mid-century enamel kitchenware. Warmer than it sounds at this saturation, and it is the one
warm ground the distance check leaves.

**Separation: stacked enamel plates.** Content sits on flat panels with a hard 2px rim in a
darker tone, stacked and slightly overlapping at the corners — enamelware, not cards. The rim
is the division. No shadow, no radius beyond 2px, no hairline rules (01), no apertures (03).

**Type.** Antique wood-type condensed — the compressed, slightly irregular lettering of a
rural general-store sign, which is also what bilingual signage in the north looks like.

*Corrected during planning:* this was first written as "condensed grotesque", which collides
with Template 05's grotesque. D23 requires the type class to be unique across the set, and a
condensed grotesque is still a grotesque. Wood type is a genuinely separate class and a better
fit for painted enamel anyway.

Caps for the French name, sentence case for the English, so the two languages are visually
distinct before you read them.

**Both names, always.** Product headings carry the French name and the English name with a
rule between them, the same size. Neither is a subtitle. That typographic decision *is* the
concept.

**Photography.** Enamel, wood, snow, maple bush, a kitchen table rather than a studio. Hard
daylight. Its own set — see the photography item in `handover.md`.

**What it must not become.** Not a Parisian pâtisserie: no gold, no marble, no copperplate
script. Not folksy — no gingham, no plaid, no cartoon maple leaves. The register is a working
rural bakery with good signage, not a heritage gift shop.

## 5. Voice: bilingual, and neither language is a translation of the other

The sixth narrator: **the granddaughter who grew up behind the counter**, switching language
mid-sentence without announcing it, as bilingual people actually do.

Not "the small honest bakery explaining its limits" — that voice was unintentionally applied
to all five existing templates at once and had to be unpicked. See `copy-references.md`.

The reference for the bilingual move is **Art-Is-In Bakery**, Ottawa, which named itself so
that "art-is-in" sounds like *artisan* in French. A name that works in both languages without
being translated is exactly the register.

**The French Baker** (Ottawa, 1995) is a useful counter-example: it writes in English with
French words sprinkled in — *savoir-faire* — which is not bilingual, it is English with
seasoning. Its motto, *"Keep it simple, keep it good,"* is worth borrowing as a standard of
terseness, not as a sentence.

## 6. Accessibility

- `lang` set correctly per element wherever the two languages sit adjacent — a screen reader
  must not read *tarte au sucre* with an English voice. This matters more here than anywhere
  else in the set, because every product heading is bilingual.
- The language switch changes `lang` on `<html>` and is a real control with an accessible
  name in **both** languages, not a flag icon. Flags are countries, not languages, and
  Franco-Ontarians are not French.
- Wheat gold is mid-tone: expect the same two-surface discipline Template 05 arrived at —
  dark ink on the ground for body, accents confined to panels. Measure before asserting.
- Blueberry carries white at 9.9:1.

## 7. Pages

The usual nine plus the style guide. Two additions specific to this template:

- **The shop's year** — not Barcelona's festival wheel but the Ontario one: maple season
  (March), *fête franco-ontarienne* (25 September), *temps des fêtes* with tourtière and
  bûche. Lighter than 06's calendar; a section, not the organising principle.
- **A glossary**, because the two-names idea creates genuine questions. Short, factual, and
  the place the 1900 currants fact lives.

## 8. Open questions

1. **Hearst** is a real, strongly francophone Ontario town. Confirm the invented business name
   collides with nothing real before shipping.
2. **Which language leads** on first load. Current plan: French, with the switch visible
   immediately — it is a Franco-Ontarian bakery, and leading in English would undercut the
   whole idea. Worth putting to the client.
3. **How much of the butter-tart argument to stage.** It is charming and it is a real
   division; it should not tip into novelty.
4. **Tourtière is savoury.** The set has sold only sweet things so far. Check the shared
   commerce layer handles a savoury line with no inscription and no occasion without
   special-casing.
